Section 01 // Core Concept

From cell to system

Bigger organisms need organisation — specialised cells grouped together to do a job efficiently.

Cell→Tissue→Organ→Organ system→Organism

Tissue

A group of similar cells working together, e.g. muscle tissue or glandular tissue (which makes digestive juices).

Organ

Different tissues working together, e.g. the stomach contains muscle tissue and glandular tissue.

What is digestion?

Breaking down large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble ones that can be absorbed into the blood.

Two kinds of digestion

Mechanical — chewing and churning breaks food into smaller pieces. Chemical — enzymes break down molecules.

Section 03 // Enzyme Lab

Find the sweet spot

Enzymes are proteins that speed up digestion — but only work well in their optimum temperature and pH. Push either too far and the enzyme denatures (its shape is destroyed) and stops working.

Section 04 // Encrypted: GCSE Stretch

Advanced data

GCSE · Higher tier extension
🔒 Lock and key, bile & food tests
Lock and key model: an enzyme's active site has a specific shape that only fits one particular substrate — like a key fitting one lock. This is why amylase can't break down protein.
Bile is made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder, and released into the small intestine. It's alkaline, so it neutralises the acid arriving from the stomach — and it emulsifies fats into tiny droplets, giving lipase a much bigger surface area to work on.
Food tests (required practical): Iodine turns blue-black for starch. Benedict's solution turns brick-red when heated with sugars. Biuret reagent turns purple/lilac for protein. Shaking with ethanol then water gives a cloudy white emulsion for lipids.

The stomach's low pH (around pH 2, from hydrochloric acid) is the optimum environment for protease (pepsin) — but it would denature amylase, which is why amylase only works in the mouth and small intestine, not the stomach.
