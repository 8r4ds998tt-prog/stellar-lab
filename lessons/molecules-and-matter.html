Section 01 // Core Concept

The particle model

Solids, liquids and gases are made of the same particles. The difference is how they're arranged and how much energy they have.

Solid

Regular pattern, touching. Particles vibrate in fixed positions.

Liquid

Close together but random. Particles slide past each other.

Gas

Far apart, random. Particles move fast in all directions.

Density

ρ = m ÷ V, the mass in each unit of volume (kg/m³ or g/cm³). Solids and liquids are dense because their particles are packed together. Gases are about 1000× less dense because their particles are spread out.

Changes of state

Melting, freezing, boiling, evaporating, condensing and subliming are physical changes. They can be reversed, and mass is conserved because no particles are made or destroyed.

Section 04 // Out in the World

Particles at work

🧊

Ice floats

Water is unusual: when it freezes, its particles lock into an open pattern, so ice is less dense than water. Ponds freeze from the top down, which keeps fish alive underneath.

🥵

Sweating cools you

When sweat evaporates, the fastest particles escape. That lowers the average kinetic energy of what's left, so your skin cools.

♨️

Steam burns are worse

Steam at 100 °C releases its huge latent heat of vaporisation as it condenses on your skin, before it cools any further.

🌊

Water heats slowly

Water has a very high specific heat capacity, which is why the sea stays cool on a hot day and makes a great coolant in car engines.

🔒 Internal energy, heat equations & gas pressure
Internal energy is the total kinetic energy plus potential energy of all the particles in a system. Heating either raises the temperature (more kinetic energy) or changes the state (more potential energy), but not both at once.
Specific heat capacity: ΔE = m c Δθ, the energy needed to raise 1 kg by 1 °C. Water: c = 4200 J/kg °C.
Specific latent heat: E = m L, the energy needed to change the state of 1 kg with no change in temperature. Water: Lf (fusion, melting) = 334 000 J/kg and Lv (vaporisation, boiling) = 2 260 000 J/kg. Lv is about 7× bigger because boiling has to pull the particles completely apart.
Gas pressure: gas particles collide with the walls of their container and exert a force on them. At a fixed volume, a higher temperature means faster particles, which hit the walls harder and more often, so the pressure goes up.
Triple onlyBoyle's law: for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, p × V = constant. Squash it into half the volume and the pressure doubles.
Triple only · Higher tierDoing work on a gas (like pumping a bike tyre) also raises its internal energy, so its temperature rises.
