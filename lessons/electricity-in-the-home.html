Section 01 // Core Concept

AC, DC and the UK mains

Not all electricity behaves the same way — and mains electricity is far more dangerous than a battery.

Direct current (DC)

Flows in one direction only. Batteries and cells supply DC.

Alternating current (AC)

Reverses direction repeatedly. UK mains electricity is AC, alternating at 50 Hz (50 times a second).

UK mains supply

UK mains electricity is about 230 V — more than 20× a typical AA battery, which is why it's so dangerous.

Three-core cable

Most home appliances connect with three wires: live, neutral and earth — each with its own job.

Section 03 // Staying Safe

Why every wire matters

🟤

Live wire (brown)

Carries the alternating potential difference from the supply — around 230 V relative to earth. It's the most dangerous wire to touch.

🔵

Neutral wire (blue)

Completes the circuit, carrying current back to the supply at close to 0 V.

🟢🟡

Earth wire (green & yellow)

A safety wire connected to the case of an appliance. If the live wire touches the case, current flows to earth instead of through you.

🧯

Fuse

A thin wire that melts and breaks the circuit if the current gets too high — cutting off the live wire before a fire or shock can happen.

Section 04 // Encrypted: GCSE Stretch

Advanced data

GCSE · Higher tier extension
🔒 Power, energy costs & the National Grid
Power equations: P = V × I and P = I² × R. Rearranging the first gives I = P ÷ V — exactly what the Fuse Selector calculates.
Energy transferred: E = P × t (in joules, with t in seconds). Energy companies bill in kilowatt-hours (kWh): 1 kWh is 1 kW of power used for 1 hour.
Double insulation: some appliances (like a hairdryer with a plastic case) have no earth wire at all — the plastic casing itself can never become live, shown by a "double square" symbol.
The National Grid transmits electricity at very high voltage and correspondingly low current. Since power loss in the cables is P = I² × R, a lower current dramatically cuts wasted energy. Step-up transformers raise the voltage for transmission; step-down transformers lower it again before it reaches homes.

Worked example: a 3000 W kettle on a 230 V supply draws I = P ÷ V = 3000 ÷ 230 ≈ 13 A — right at the limit of a standard UK socket, which is why kettles need the 13 A fuse.
