Section 01 // Core Concept

Three ways to bond

Atoms bond so that they end up with a full outer shell of electrons. How they do that depends on what they're bonding with.

⚡ Ionic

A metal and a non-metal. Electrons are transferred from one atom to the other, forming charged ions that attract each other.

🔗 Covalent

Two (or more) non-metals. Atoms share pairs of electrons so both get a full outer shell.

🌊 Metallic

Metal atoms only. Outer electrons become delocalised — free to roam through a lattice of positive ions.

Why it matters

The type of bond decides the structure a substance forms — and the structure decides its melting point, hardness and whether it conducts electricity.

Section 03 // Structure & Properties

The bond decides the behaviour

Four structures show up again and again on the exam — match the structure to what it does.

🧊

Giant ionic lattice

e.g. NaCl. High melting point (strong attraction in every direction). Conducts only when molten or dissolved, when the ions are free to move.

💧

Simple molecular

e.g. Cl₂, H₂O, CO₂. Low melting/boiling point — the covalent bonds are strong, but the forces between molecules are weak and easy to overcome. Doesn't conduct.

💎

Giant covalent

e.g. diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide. Every atom is covalently bonded to its neighbours — extremely high melting point and very hard (except graphite).

🥇

Giant metallic

e.g. iron, copper. Conducts electricity (delocalised electrons carry charge) and is malleable — layers of ions slide without breaking the bond.

Section 04 // Encrypted: GCSE Stretch

Advanced data

GCSE · Higher tier extension
🔒 Carbon's allotropes, alloys & polymers
Diamond: every carbon atom forms 4 covalent bonds to neighbours — rigid, transparent, extremely hard, and does not conduct because every electron is locked in a bond.
Graphite: each carbon only bonds to 3 others, forming layers. The 4th electron from each atom becomes delocalised between the layers, so graphite conducts — and the layers can slide over each other, making it soft and slippery.
Graphene & fullerenes: graphene is a single layer of graphite (one atom thick) — incredibly strong and a great conductor. Fullerenes are carbon atoms arranged as hollow cages or tubes (e.g. buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀), used in nanotechnology and drug delivery.
Alloys: mixtures of a metal with other elements (e.g. steel = iron + carbon). The different-sized atoms distort the regular layers, so the layers can't slide as easily — alloys are usually harder than the pure metal.

Polymers are long chains of repeating covalent units. The covalent bonds along the chain are strong, but the forces between separate chains are weak — so polymers melt more easily than giant covalent structures, but have higher melting points than small molecules like Cl₂.
