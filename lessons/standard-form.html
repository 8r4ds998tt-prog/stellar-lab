Section 01 // Core Concept

A × 10ⁿ — the two-part code

Standard form packs any number into exactly two parts. A (the coefficient) is always between 1 and 10. n (the power) counts how many times you multiply or divide by 10.

The coefficient A

Always satisfies 1 ≤ A < 10 — a single digit before the decimal point. If it's not, adjust the power of 10.

Positive power n

Large numbers: n is positive.
3,400,000 = 3.4 × 10⁶ — the decimal moved 6 places left.

Negative power n

Small numbers: n is negative.
0.000054 = 5.4 × 10⁻⁵ — the decimal moved 5 places right.

Converting back

Multiply A by 10ⁿ. For positive n, move the decimal right; for negative n, move left.

Section 03 // Science Scale

Numbers scientists actually use

🌍

Earth's mass

About 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg — a 1 followed by 24 zeros would fill this page ten times.

🦠

Bacteria size

A typical bacterium is roughly 2 × 10⁻⁶ m across — two millionths of a metre.

💡

Speed of light

3 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum — standard form makes this easy to write and compare.

⚛️

Proton diameter

Around 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ m — a femtometre. Negative powers handle the tiny.

Section 04 // Encrypted: GCSE Stretch

Advanced data

🔒 Calculating in standard form
Multiplying: multiply the A values, add the powers. (3 × 10⁴) × (2 × 10³) = 6 × 10⁷. If the A product ≥ 10, adjust: 12 × 10⁵ = 1.2 × 10⁶.
Dividing: divide the A values, subtract the powers. (8 × 10⁶) ÷ (4 × 10²) = 2 × 10⁴.
Adding/subtracting: convert to the same power first, then add/subtract the A values. 3 × 10⁵ + 2 × 10⁴ = 30 × 10⁴ + 2 × 10⁴ = 32 × 10⁴ = 3.2 × 10⁵.

On a calculator: the EXP or ×10ˣ button enters the power. 3.4 EXP 6 stores 3.4 × 10⁶. Don't type × 10 separately — you'll get the wrong answer.
